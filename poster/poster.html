What a Medical AI Model’s Internal Activity Shows When a Hint Silently Changes Its Answer

Temesgen Tewolde
Duke University · AIPI 590: Explainable AI
Abstract

Earlier studies [1, 2], including our own [5], showed that AI models often switch answers because of a hint in the prompt, while their written reasoning never mentions it. This project looks inside the model to see how that happens. On 375 medical exam questions with MedGemma 4B, we found that the hint is carried inside the model mainly as “which answer letter was suggested.” A classifier that reads the model’s internal activity spots hint-following far better than tools that read the written reasoning, but it reacts to any answer that matches the hint, not specifically to hiding. Steering the model’s internal activity fixes one-word answers but not step-by-step ones, and the classifier does not catch prompt-injection attacks.

What is new
  1. We locate where the hint’s effect is carried inside the model: features that stand for the suggested answer letter. We found none that stand for who gave the hint.
  2. We build a classifier from internal activity that never sees the hint, and test it on hint styles it was not trained on.
  3. We show what that classifier really detects: agreement with the hint, not hiding.
  4. With plans fixed in advance, we test whether steering fixes the problem and whether the same signal flags prompt injection. Neither works.

Methods
  • Model: MedGemma 4B, Google’s open medical model. Gemma Scope 2 breaks its internal activity into readable features.
  • Questions: 375 four-option medical licensing exam questions (MedQA). With no hint the model gets 64.8% right.
  • Hints: each question is asked with no hint, a harmless filler sentence (the baseline), and four hint styles: a metadata tag, “Hint: the answer is X”, an attending physician’s opinion, and a patient’s insistence.
  • Labels: a second AI model (Qwen 27B) checks whether the reasoning mentions the hint and must quote the exact words. Hidden means the model followed the hint and never mentioned it.
  • Classifier: a simple model trained on internal activity and tested only on questions it never saw.
  • Score: AUROC, where 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect.
  • Steering: we found the internal direction that separates following from ignoring the hint, then removed it or pushed against it. Success rules were written down before running.
Results
1. How often hints change answers
  • Every hint style changed more answers than the filler sentence (4%). The metadata tag changed 64% of answers and was never mentioned.

Share of answers that switched to the hinted (wrong) option. Dashed line: filler sentence. Bars show 95% confidence intervals. Times the reasoning mentioned the hint: 0/206, 6/175, 14/95, 1/57.

Results (continued)
2. Where the hint is carried, and detecting it
  • The features that carry the hint stand for the suggested answer letter. Switching off the top 10 lowered hint-following in one-word answers from 87% to 75%, but step-by-step answers did not change (35 of 40 still followed the hint).
  • The classifier scores 0.98 and works on hint styles it never trained on (0.98–0.99). When the hint names the answer in words instead of a letter, it drops to 0.82–0.83.

How well each method separates hidden hint-following from ignored hints (1,272 answers). Classifier read after or before the reasoning. Bottom bar: the same classifier on 149 prompt-injection cases.

3. Trying to fix it by steering
  • Removing the hint direction cut hint-following in one-word answers from 45% to 28%; random directions did nothing. When the model reasons step by step, 0 of 4 methods reduced hidden hint-following on 100 new questions.

Hint-following with and without the fix, on the first set of questions. Bars show 95% confidence intervals.

4. Prompt injection
  • Hidden instructions inside the question (MPIB) changed 26% of answers, but the classifier could not tell which ones (0.48, no better than guessing).
Conclusions
0.98
how well internal activity identifies hint-following (1.0 = perfect)
0 of 4
fixes that worked when the model reasons step by step
  1. The hint’s effect shows up in the model’s internal activity even when its written reasoning says nothing.
  2. The classifier detects “the answer matches the hint,” not hiding. It also fires when the model openly mentions the hint (76% of such cases) and when the model would have chosen that answer anyway (54%). Catching real hiding needs a second check on the written reasoning.
  3. Fixes that work on one-word answers fail once the model reasons step by step; the reasoning seems to bring the hint’s effect back.
  4. The classifier did not catch prompt injection, so it should not be trusted on problems it was not trained on.
Limitations
  • One small (4B) model; English multiple-choice questions.
  • Most hints name an answer letter. When we inserted a made-up clinical finding instead, the model mentioned it in 59 of 61 cases, so there was little hiding to detect.
  • Labels come from an AI judge checked against other AI models, not doctors.
  • Gemma Scope 2 was built for the general Gemma 3 model, not MedGemma, and we examined only 4 layers.
